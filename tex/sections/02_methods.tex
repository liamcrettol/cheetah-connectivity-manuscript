\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Study area and analysis extent}
\label{sec:extent}

The study area was defined as the southern African cheetah range \parencite{weise2017distribution} plus a 100-km buffer, so that modeled paths could route around the edge of the range rather than being cut off by it. All layers were aligned to a fixed equal-area projection (Africa Albers Equal Area Conic) and a \cellsize\ snap grid, so every raster was directly comparable cell for cell. Cores were derived from the 2010--2016 cheetah density map and held constant across 2012, 2016, 2020, and 2024. The final network consisted of 23 cores and 45 selected neighboring core pairs (\figref{fig:studyarea}). A 1-km working resolution was used because it represented a compromise between the coarser 10-km occurrence baseline and the finer environmental covariates, which ranged from 30 m to 1 km. This resolution was fine enough to identify meaningful points in the landscape while avoiding the implication of a level of accuracy that the occurrence evidence alone could not provide.

\subsection{Range cores}
\label{sec:cores}

A core area was defined as a contiguous patch of cells with a density of $\geq$ 0.51 cheetahs per 100~km$^2$ \parencite{weise2017data}. This value was where the planned top-10, 20, and 30 percent density rules all converged, because it covers a large, uniform part of the density map. The final network consisted of 23 cores, which were held constant across all four temporal snapshots to avoid inventing core movement in the absence of a comparable range reassessment. The minimum core size was 500~km$^2$, roughly the home range of a territorial male or a female cheetah \parencite{melzheimer2020hubs}. The protocol had planned three minimum sizes (2,000, 1,000, and 500~km$^2$); only the 500-km$^2$ definition was carried through the analysis.

\subsection{Covariates and their temporal treatment}
\label{sec:covariates}

The temporal change in resistance came from three continuous, regularly updated layers: human development from GHSL, nighttime lights from VIIRS, and vegetation continuous fields (VCF) from MODIS (Table~\ref{tab:covariates}). Categorical land cover was used for masking and description only, because annual categorical maps can switch classes between years for reasons unrelated to overall landscape change.

Static covariates (roads, livestock, terrain, and fences) were also used. These did not contribute \textit{temporal} signal to the resistance surface; however, they did contribute \textit{spatial} signal to it. Hydrography and protected areas were used only to describe the landscape and to report protected-area coverage.

\subsection{Resistance scenarios}
\label{sec:resistance}

Resistance values were assigned to represent the relative difficulty of movement through each landscape feature. The main surface had a weight of 60\% for human pressure, 20\% for vegetation, and 20\% for terrain. Within human pressure, built-up land, roads, and livestock each received 30\%, while nighttime lights received 10\%. Fence data were incorporated into the resistance model: cells without a fence kept their resistance, and fence cells were multiplied by 25. Fences were not treated as complete barriers, because they do not fully block large carnivores \parencite{cozzi2013barriers}. Slopes of 10 degrees or less were given a resistance of 1, and slopes between 10 and 30 degrees a resistance that increased in a straight line to 10. Steeper slopes were assigned a resistance of 10, with slope calculated as the average within each 1-km cell. Alternative vegetation weights were compared because vegetation cover can influence cheetah habitat use \parencite{verschueren2024guild}, and different weighting schemes allowed a test of how strongly the modeled resistance surfaces depended on the vegetation weight \parencite{zeller2012resistance}. The weights, slope thresholds, and fence multiplier were set by the author based on the cited literature, and were not fitted to cheetah movement data.

Three human-pressure weightings (the main weighting, equal, and infrastructure-emphasis), three vegetation weights (10, 20, and 40 percent), and five fence treatments (no fences, documented fences, near-barrier, Kruger documented, and Kruger conservative) were tested. Each alternative changed one setting from the main surface, giving a main surface plus eight alternatives. The first axis changed the balance among the anthropogenic pressures, comparing the main weighting against an equal weighting and an infrastructure-emphasis weighting. The second changed how much the surface leaned on vegetation relative to anthropogenic pressure and terrain, at 10, 20, and 40 percent. The vegetation-balanced surface combined with the documented fence treatment carries the primary results, and the remaining combinations are reported as sensitivity rather than as alternative answers. This surface was chosen as the main one because it sits at the middle of the tested vegetation range and uses only fence locations that were documented. The predeclared protocol had treated all scenarios equally; a main surface was designated afterwards so that results could be reported once, with the alternatives as sensitivity. A link is only described as stable if it survives these alternatives (Section~\ref{sec:uncertainty}).

\subsection{Connectivity modelling}
\label{sec:connectivity}

For each core, a least-cost analysis in ArcGIS calculated the accumulated cost of moving through every reachable cell. The lowest accumulated cost was then used to draw paths from the starting core to each connected destination, for the 45 selected links (Section~\ref{sec:graph}). Reusing the accumulated-cost calculation for several destinations produced the same set of pairwise paths with fewer repeat calculations. Circuitscape \circuitscapeversion\ in pairwise mode \parencite{anantharaman2020circuitscape} was used to model how current spread between each pair of cores. The Circuitscape analysis included all 253 core pairs in each of the four years, for a total of 1,012 pairwise comparisons.

\subsection{Network structure}
\label{sec:graph}

The 23 core areas were represented as fixed nodes across all four temporal snapshots (2012, 2016, 2020, and 2024). Each core was linked to its three nearest neighbors by Euclidean distance, and a minimum spanning tree, which is the smallest set of links needed to keep the core network connected, was added to ensure all cores were connected. This yielded 45 straight-line links, which defined the core pairs that received least-cost paths (Section~\ref{sec:connectivity}).

Edge betweenness counts how many shortest paths between cores pass through a link. A high edge betweenness means that the link is important for holding the larger network together. The value was calculated on the 45-link network and divided by the 253 possible core pairs. To test how much the network design affected the rankings, the number of nearest neighbors was also varied from two to five.

\subsection{Temporal comparison}
\label{sec:temporal}

The same core pairs, model assumptions, and 1-km grid were compared in 2012, 2016, 2020, and 2024. This kept the comparison consistent, so that the modeled differences came only from the input layers that changed through time. Absolute change was defined as the later value minus the earlier value. Percent change was defined as that difference divided by the earlier value, multiplied by 100. A negative change therefore means a connection became easier to cross in the model.

\subsection{Protected-area coverage}
\label{sec:pa}

The August 2026 WDPA release \parencite{unepwcmc2026wdpa} was used to determine protected-area coverage, keeping terrestrial and coastal protected-area polygons, removing proposed areas (areas that are not officially protected), points, and marine-only areas, and merging overlaps. This left 2,301 records in the study extent. The protected-area data were the most recent available and were not reconstructed at each historical date. Cells inside the fixed cores were excluded, because the model forces current into and out of the cores, which would make protection look higher without describing the land between them. Cells with zero current were also excluded, because they could never enter a high-current group, so protected-area coverage was calculated only for areas that could carry modeled current between the cores. High-current cells were defined as those above the 80th, 90th, 95th, and 99th percentiles of modeled current, and their protected share was compared with that of all usable outside-core cells. No significance test is reported, because the result depended on the assumed size of the spatial blocks.

\subsection{Stability and uncertainty}
\label{sec:uncertainty}

A link was treated as stable only if it passed three checks, and as uncertain if it failed one or more of them. First, under both alternative human-pressure weightings (equal and infrastructure-emphasis), at least 80 percent of the route had to stay within 5 km of the main route. Second, under the near-barrier fence treatment, the link's modeled cost could not rise by more than 20 percent, and at least 80 percent of its route had to stay within 5 km of the main route. Third, the direction of change from 2012 to 2024 had to stay the same under all three vegetation weightings. Separately, route location was checked from year to year by measuring how much of each least-cost path stayed within 1 km of the same path in the other snapshots.

\subsection{Independent plausibility assessment}
\label{sec:validation}

All inputs to the connectivity model are static. The cores are fixed from historical records, the landscape is represented by four snapshots, and the modeled paths are estimates rather than observed routes. To assess whether this static representation bears any relation to how cheetahs actually move, the model was compared with independent GPS tracking data from free-ranging cheetahs. This comparison is a plausibility check rather than a validation, because no resistance value was fitted to these data. The tracking data consist of 3,165 locations from nine free-ranging cheetahs (seven males and two females) collared near Thabazimbi, Limpopo Province, South Africa, between September 2003 and November 2008 \parencite{marnewick2017ewtdata, marnewick2017tracking}. The tracked area lies inside the study extent but outside every core, so these animals were moving through the landscape between cores, which is the part of the map the model makes claims about. The data are also independent of the cores, as none of these collar locations appear in the range archive from which the cores were derived \parencite{weise2017data}.

Seventeen records without a complete date were removed, and the remaining locations were thinned to the earliest fix per animal per calendar day, leaving 1,006 locations. Locations from the same animal on consecutive days were then paired into day-to-day moves. Moves shorter than 1~km were excluded because both ends fall within the same 1~km cell, which left 549 moves with a median distance of approximately 4~km. Each move was compared with 20 alternative moves the same animal could have made instead, following the logic of step-selection analysis, a method that compares observed movements with the alternatives available from the same starting point \parencite{fortin2005wolves, thurfjell2014ssf}. Each alternative began at the same location as the real move, covered a distance drawn from that animal's own movements, and headed in a random direction. Because every alternative began where the real move began, the comparison reflects the animal's choice of destination rather than the general character of the area it lived in. Modeled resistance was read from the 2012 surfaces, the snapshot closest to the tracking period, at the end point of each move and averaged along the straight line between its start and end. For each move, the share of alternatives that reached higher resistance than the real move was recorded. If modeled resistance had no bearing on movement, this share would be approximately 0.50. Shares were averaged within each animal so that each of the nine animals counted once, and a 95\% interval was derived by resampling the animals 10,000 times.

A broader comparison was also made between the 1,006 daily locations and ten random points per location drawn within the convex hull of all locations, repeated with the hull widened by 20~km. To see which inputs carried any result, the step comparison was repeated for each input layer on its own and for the main surface with each layer left out, and it was repeated on all nine resistance surfaces. The number of mapped roads crossed by each real move was also compared with its alternatives. These checks were added after the planned comparison was run, and none of them was used to change any weight or the choice of main surface. A weaker, non-independent comparison used the occurrence records of the range archive itself, thinned to one record per 10-km cell for 2010--2012 and for 2013--2016.

\subsection{Monitoring prioritisation}
\label{sec:mcda}

For each of the 45 links, six measurements that could be used to prioritize monitoring were compiled and kept separate rather than combined into a single score, because no evidence-based way to weigh them against each other was available. The first was the edge betweenness of the link, which measures how important it is to the overall connectivity of the network. The second was the change in modeled resistance between 2012 and 2024, which indicates how much the landscape changed in terms of movement difficulty. The third, fourth, and fifth were the three stability checks (Section~\ref{sec:uncertainty}), which show how robust each link is to changes in model assumptions. The sixth was the straight-line length of the link, since longer links must cross more of the landscape. The protocol had also planned a monitoring index for every 10-km cell; that index was not built, so the monitoring-priority hypothesis could not be tested.

\subsection{Software and reproducibility}
\label{sec:software}

ArcGIS Pro \arcgisversion\ (build \arcgisbuild) and ArcGIS Python \pythonversion\ were used for most of the spatial processing, Julia \juliaversion\ and Circuitscape.jl \circuitscapeversion\ for the connectivity modeling, and Google Earth Engine to supply and align the satellite data. Most ArcGIS processing used Python scripts rather than ModelBuilder. An AI assistant (Claude, Anthropic) was used to help write and debug the analysis scripts and to draft the text, which the author reviewed, revised, and verified against the source outputs. The author takes full responsibility for the content.
