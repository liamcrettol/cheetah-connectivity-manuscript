\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

The southern African cheetah (\textit{Acinonyx jubatus jubatus}) has lost much of its historical range, and most of its mapped 2010--2016 range lies outside protected areas. Habitat suitability in the region has been widely studied, but change over time in connectivity between known range areas has not been measured. This study modeled potential structural connectivity among 23 range cores from the 2010--2016 range assessment. The cores were held fixed across four snapshots (2012, 2016, 2020, and 2024), so every change reported reflects the landscape rather than the cheetahs. A resistance surface built from human pressure, vegetation, slope, and veterinary fences was used to model least-cost paths and circuit-theory current, and its assumptions were varied across nine surfaces. Across all 253 core pairs, the median modeled cost of moving between cores changed by only $-1.2$ percent; 161 pairs became easier and 92 became harder. Outside the cores, 23 percent of the land above the 80th percentile of modeled current was protected, against 31 percent of the comparison area, although protection rose to 45 percent in the top 1 percent. Of 45 selected links, 32 remained stable under the sensitivity tests and 13 were uncertain. In GPS tracking data from nine free-ranging cheetahs, daily moves ended on lower modeled resistance than comparable alternative moves for seven of the nine animals, a modest lean carried mostly by roads. These results suggest that change was mixed rather than a broad decline, and that land outside protected areas carries much of the modeled connectivity.
