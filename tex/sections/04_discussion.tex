\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Connectivity change and comparison with previous work}

The modeled ease of movement between cores changed only slightly from 2012 to 2024. Across all 253 core pairs, the median cost fell by about 1.2 percent, and individual connections changed in both directions rather than declining together. These results describe landscape structure and do not show where cheetahs actually moved. The changes that did occur were concentrated in a minority of links, with five links carrying almost half of the total increase in modeled cost, but they did not fall disproportionately on the links most important to the network. Because the cores were held fixed and most input layers were static, any modeled change came from the three time-varying layers: built-up area, nighttime lights, and vegetation cover. The overall picture is therefore one of mixed change rather than a broad decline.

The general approach used here resembles regional connectivity studies of elephants and of several species across the Kavango--Zambezi Transfrontier Conservation Area \parencite{naidoo2024kaza,brennan2020multispecies}, and work showing how veterinary fences alter large-mammal movement \parencite{osipova2018fencing}. Similar mapping tools can be applied to different species, but the resistance values themselves cannot simply be transferred, because each species responds differently to roads, vegetation, fences, and people. Veterinary fences are a clear example. In northern Botswana many fence sections are already in disrepair, and a recent disease-risk assessment found that removing several sections would not raise the risk of the main transboundary livestock diseases, prompting consultations on opening low-risk sections \parencite{rosen2026fences}. This supports treating fences as raising resistance rather than as complete barriers, as in the present model, and it makes the fence scenarios relevant to a current policy question. The model does not, however, show what removing a particular fence would mean for cheetahs, because no fence-removal scenario was run.

\subsection{Protected areas and conservation context}

The strongest concentrations of modeled current often reached protected areas, but the broader land connecting them was less protected than the study area as a whole. This suggests that maintaining modeled connectivity between cheetah range cores depends on farms, communal land, and other land outside formal protected areas, not only on parks. The pattern does not mean that protected areas have failed. Parks are often located on land of lower agricultural value, which may partly shape where both protection and modeled current occur. The result is also descriptive: the significance of the difference depended on the assumed size of the spatial blocks, so it should be read as a pattern rather than a tested effect.

\subsection{Priority links and field-survey needs}

Links that stayed stable under several assumptions are the strongest candidates for conservation attention. Thirty-two of the 45 links met that standard, which is most of the network rather than the small subset originally expected, so the stability screen narrows priorities less than intended. Agreement between scenarios is also not complete proof, because the scenarios share many of the same input layers. The 13 uncertain links are better treated as places where field information could change the result. No combined score ranks individual survey cells, so an organization could use these links to choose general survey areas and then adjust the order by access, cost, safety, and local knowledge. Link 17--24 is a clear first candidate: it was the only link holding two parts of the three-neighbor network together, yet its mapped route was highly unstable and had no overlap in 2020.

\subsection{How far the tracking check supports the model}

In one district, free-ranging cheetahs moving from one day to the next tended to reach ground that the model rates as easier than the ground they could have reached instead. This held for seven of the nine animals and on all nine resistance surfaces. The check could have failed: had the animals chosen higher-resistance ground, the result would have pointed the other way. The effect was modest, however, with real moves beating about 56 percent of their alternatives against 50 percent by chance, so the result is consistent with the model rather than a close match.

Most of the signal came from roads. Road density alone performed about as well as the full surface, and the animals crossed mapped roads less often than their alternative moves did, in line with the road avoidance reported for GPS-collared cheetahs in the Maasai Mara \parencite{klaassen2018edge}. Vegetation on its own leaned slightly the wrong way, and modeled current showed no clear pattern. Cheetahs in this ranchland may use denser bush for cover and hunting, and the tracks predate the 2012 vegetation layer; either could explain the vegetation result, but neither was tested. No weight was changed in response to this check, which keeps it independent of the model. The check does not show that the model holds elsewhere, because it covers nine animals in one part of South Africa, and it says nothing about change over time, because the tracks end in 2008. It also could not test fences, because none are mapped near the tracks and the private game fences in the area are not in the model. The planned comparison with camera-trap data from central-eastern Namibia \parencite{verschueren2024guild} was not carried out, and independent movement data from fenced and more developed areas would be the most useful next test.
