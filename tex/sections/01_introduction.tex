\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The southern African cheetah has had its range substantially reduced, and its mapped 2010--2016 range is estimated to lie mostly on unprotected land \parencite{weise2017distribution}. Cheetah range on protected land is governed by centralized park-management initiatives \parencite{buk2018metapopulation}. Unprotected land poses a different type of challenge, with landowners, local communities, and governmental entities all involved. This paper examines landscape connectivity change between 2012 and 2024. The core range of cheetahs was fixed at the 2010--2016 baseline, so modeled changes in permeability reflect landscape change rather than change in the core range itself. Throughout, \structconn\ refers to modeled landscape permeability between fixed range cores. It is not evidence of cheetah movement, dispersal, or gene flow. Where a location is described as a pinch point, this means a location of concentrated modeled current or a link-level bottleneck, not an observed crossing.

Previous research in the region has predominantly focused on habitat suitability, a topic that has been widely studied. The objective of this study was not to develop an additional suitability model, but rather to identify and evaluate candidate spatial ``pinch points'' (i.e., constricted linkages) among core areas. The study most comparable to the present work is a recent multi-species connectivity assessment of the Kavango--Zambezi Transfrontier Conservation Area \parencite{sousa2026kaza}; however, that analysis excluded the cheetah because of insufficient data. Data limitations were therefore considered and mitigated to the extent possible in this study, and they provide both the primary justification for this paper and its main constraint. The approach combines range cores fixed at a historical baseline, repeated landscape snapshots from 2012 to 2024, and an independent check against GPS tracking data.

Two primary connectivity approaches are commonly used to model these kinds of frameworks: least-cost connectivity and circuit-theoretic connectivity \parencite{adriaensen2003leastcost,mcrae2008circuit}. The least-cost approach links core areas by selecting the route with the lowest cumulative ``cost,'' meaning the path of least resistance between cores, based on the resistance assigned to each landscape factor. In contrast, the circuit-theoretic approach represents the landscape as a resistor network, sends a ``current'' through it, and highlights redundancy across the broader landscape, relying on the same resistance values. A key limitation of both methods is that a resistance value must first be assigned to every cell (1 km in this study). \textcite{zeller2012resistance} demonstrated that even reasonable alternative assumptions about resistance values can influence results more strongly than the choice of connectivity method itself. For that reason, rather than treating one resistance surface as definitive, the weights were varied along two axes (Section~\ref{sec:resistance}). This reduces how much the results depend on any one choice of weights.

The objectives were to (1) measure how modeled connectivity between fixed range cores changed from 2012 to 2024, and (2) identify which connections stayed robust under different model assumptions and how much of the connecting land is protected. Four hypotheses follow from these objectives.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Temporal-change hypothesis.} Temporal change in connectivity is expected to be strongest among a minority of links, and the links that are most important to the network are expected to experience the greatest loss between 2012 and 2024. The importance of a link is measured by its betweenness in the network, which is the number of shortest paths between cores that pass through it. A link with a high betweenness is therefore more important to the network than a link with a low betweenness. This was compared among the 45 selected links. This hypothesis would be refuted if loss were spread roughly evenly across links, or fell mainly on links with low betweenness.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Protected-area-coverage hypothesis.} High-value connectivity areas are those with the highest modeled current. These areas are expected to be located disproportionately outside formal protected areas. This would be refuted if the unprotected share of high-current area were not greater than in the comparison area, or if the result did not hold across current thresholds.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Link-stability hypothesis.} The stability of a link is defined as its ability to maintain connectivity under varying model assumptions. Some links may be more sensitive to changes in the landscape because of their location or the surrounding land use (e.g., a link surrounded by agricultural land may be more sensitive to land-use change than a link surrounded by protected areas). A small group of links is expected to remain important under all resistance scenarios, while other links are expected to be sensitive to the model changes. This would be refuted if agreement between scenarios were uniformly high, meaning the sensitivity tests added nothing, or uniformly low, meaning no stable group exists.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Monitoring-priority hypothesis.} The active monitoring of cheetahs in the wild is as challenging as it is important, and the areas chosen for monitoring determine how efficiently conservation funding is used. The highest-priority places to monitor are expected to be where network importance overlaps with old or sparse cheetah records and fast landscape change.
