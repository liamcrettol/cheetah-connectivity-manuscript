Two primary connectivity approaches are commonly used to model these kinds of frameworks: least-cost connectivity and circuit-theoretic connectivity (\cite{adriaensen2003leastcost}; \cite{mcrae2008circuit})\note{change to one \texttt{\textbackslash parencite\{adriaensen2003leastcost,mcrae2008circuit\}} so the brackets come out right in every layout}. The least-cost approach links core areas by selecting the route with the lowest cumulative “cost,” meaning the path of least resistance between cores, based on weight values assigned to each multivariate resistor. In contrast, the circuit-theoretic approach represents the landscape as a resistor network, sends a “current” through it, and highlights redundancy across the broader landscape, relying on the same multivariate resistors. A key limitation of both methods is that \sugrep{a resistance ``score'' or ``weight'' must first be assigned}{you must first assign a resistance “score” or “weight”}{impersonal; papers avoid ``you''} to every cell (1 km in my case). \textcite{zeller2012resistance} demonstrated that even reasonable alternative assumptions about resistance values can influence results more strongly than the choice of connectivity method itself. For that reason, rather than treating one resistance surface as definitive, I varied the weights along two axes (Section~\ref{sec:resistance}). This \sugrep{reduces how much the results depend on any one choice of weights}{helps to minimize bias from the selection of resistor weights}{``minimize bias'' overclaims; varying weights shows sensitivity, it does not remove bias}.
